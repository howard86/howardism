## Summary

The one concrete institutional mechanism in [Zuckerberg's August 2026 superintelligence manifesto](/articles/balance-of-power-superintelligence) (`prediction` tier — a proposal, unmeasured, from the CEO of a company it would bind): **frontier labs should give the government intermediate training checkpoints of new models, plus technical staff, rather than waiting for a finished model to be reviewed.**

He states it twice, in near-identical words, under two different risk headings — cybersecurity and government tyranny — which is the tell for how load-bearing it is:

> "frontier AI labs should share intermediate training checkpoints of new models for government use and review rather than waiting until training has completed. This way the government will have early access to the most powerful models and an army of capable engineers to identify and patch security issues."

The design goal is explicit and is the whole point: *"These steps would accelerate safe deployment without slowing the pace of model innovation or meaningfully delaying individuals' access to this technology."* Oversight that costs zero release latency.

## What makes it structurally different from the other proposals in this corpus

The vault now holds five distinct answers to "who checks a frontier model before the world gets it," and this one occupies a corner none of the others do.

| | Reviewer | Timing | Output | Release latency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [RSP gating](/articles/responsible-scaling-policy-evals) | the lab itself | pre-release | deploy / don't, with safeguards | whatever the eval takes |
| [Cross-lab review](/articles/cross-lab-pre-release-review) | competitors | 1–2 weeks pre-release | recommendation to delay | 1–2 weeks, by construction |
| [Standards Body](/articles/frontier-ai-standards-body) | an industry-funded SRO + federal agencies / National Labs | up to 30 days pre-release, plus post-release | pass/fail for the US market, eventually mandatory | ≤30 days while voluntary, then whatever the protocol takes |
| [Pause verification](/articles/frontier-pause-verification) | multilateral, instrumented | continuous, on training | a credible stop | unbounded, that is the point |
| **Checkpoint sharing** | government engineers | **mid-training** | **hardened infrastructure** | **zero, by design** |
| [Compute-allocation pacing](/articles/domestic-frontier-pacing) | embedded third-party auditors | **continuous, on compute shares** | **compliance with an allocation floor — no model verdict at all** | zero for any given release; caps the rate of capability growth instead |

*(Standards Body row added 2026-08-12. It is the **earliest** of the three 2026 pre-release proposals by publication date — 07-14, against Musk's 07-29 and this page's 08-10 — so the design space was populated from the gating end inward, not outward from it. The pacing row, added the same day from a 08-05 source, is the only entry that never evaluates a model: it regulates the **inputs**, which is why it shares this page's zero-release-latency property while keeping a power this page's design explicitly gives up — an allocation floor set to 100% external inference is a full stop on capability advancement.)*

Two of the four cells are the novelty. **Mid-training** is earlier than anyone else proposes — a checkpoint is not a product and has no release date, so access to it cannot itself be a gate. And the **output is not a verdict**: the government is not asked to say yes or no to a release; it is handed capability to point at its own critical systems. The proposal converts oversight from a permission problem into a capability-transfer problem.

That is a real answer to the expertise-asymmetry objection that [Musk raises against regulators](/articles/cross-lab-pre-release-review) ("it's quite difficult for someone in the government who doesn't have a deep technical understanding… to know whether something should be released"). Musk resolves it by relocating the judgment to competitors; Zuckerberg resolves it by giving the government the model and the engineers and never asking it to judge. Musk's mechanism keeps a delay lever and a government escalation tier; Zuckerberg's has neither.

He is explicit that this is a rejection of process: *"I think it will be more productive to have a close proactive collaboration between frontier labs and the government rather than a rigid process and review timeline that is followed in all cases."* The stated reason is competitive: *"Any policy that slows American model releases — even by a month — could add significant risk to American leadership,"* against a claimed leadership margin so thin that *"maintaining even a two-month advantage is incredibly valuable."*

## The unanswered question the mechanism creates

Everything the proposal delivers is downstream of an assumption it never argues: that **a government holding a frontier checkpoint uses it to defend and not to gate**. There is no described mechanism preventing the second — no statutory bar, no contract, no structural reason a government with early access to a capability it finds alarming would decline to act on it. The proposal's zero-latency property is a *design intent*, not an enforced property, and it is the intent of the party being overseen.

Two further gaps, both unaddressed in the text:

- **The checkpoint is itself a proliferation surface.** The argument for early access is that capability in defenders' hands hardens systems. The same checkpoint in the hands of whoever compromises the government's copy is the [unbounded-elicitation](/articles/open-weight-elicitation-irreversibility) problem with a smaller distribution list. Zuckerberg's cyber section argues the defender-advantage case at length and never applies it to the artifact his own proposal moves.
- **"An army of capable engineers"** is asserted, not sourced. The proposal requires the government to field enough frontier-competent staff to do real hardening work on a pre-release checkpoint. No corpus source establishes that this staff exists.

## Reading it as positioning

The proposal is the safety-facing half of a piece whose other half argues at length that restricting capability is the actual danger. It is offered as the alternative to the policies Zuckerberg wants rejected — it is what labs should do *instead of* release delays, capability restrictions, and rigid review timelines. Judged on its own terms it is a genuine and specific commitment, more concrete than anything else in the manifesto. Judged as governance, its distinguishing feature is that it is the only mechanism in the table above that cannot, by construction, stop anything.

- [Cross-Lab Pre-Release Review](/articles/cross-lab-pre-release-review) — the nearest rival mechanism, from the same corner of the debate a fortnight earlier: same expertise-asymmetry premise, opposite conclusion about who should hold the delay lever

- [Domestic Frontier Pacing](/articles/domestic-frontier-pacing) — the only other zero-release-latency design in the table, reached from the opposite side: this one costs nothing because it produces no verdict, that one because it regulates compute shares rather than releases. The difference is that an allocation floor can still be set to 100% external inference, which is a total stop — so it achieves this page's design goal without giving up the power this page gives up
- [Frontier AI Standards Body](/articles/frontier-ai-standards-body) — the maximal opposite in the same table: a standing body whose review eventually becomes a condition of US-market deployment, where this proposal's defining property is that it cannot stop anything. The one thing they share is the omission — neither says who adjudicates a contested assessment, though this page reaches that state by having no verdict to contest

## Open Questions

- Has any frontier lab actually transferred a pre-release checkpoint to a government body, on any terms? The proposal is stated as a recommendation to the industry, including implicitly to Meta itself; whether Meta has done it is not claimed.
- Does a government holding a frontier checkpoint mid-training, in practice, stay out of the release decision? The proposal's zero-latency property depends entirely on the answer and offers no mechanism to secure it. Trigger: any first instance of such an arrangement being disclosed.
