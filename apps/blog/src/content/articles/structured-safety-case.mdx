## Summary

The **safety case** is the argument a developer makes that its systems are unlikely to cause catastrophic harm — not the evidence, but the structure that turns evidence into a conclusion. [Anthropic's](/articles/anthropic) second RSP [Risk Report](/articles/responsible-scaling-policy-evals) (published under RSP v3.4, coverage date **2026-07-15**) is the corpus's first worked example of one written **explicitly**: a numbered decomposition, per-claim confidence qualifiers, declared aggregation rules, and a limitations section naming how the argument could be wrong.

Anthropic frames it as a deliberate upgrade in kind, and hedges accordingly: *"This is an attempt at making a more explicit and structured argument than in previous risk reports. Some arguments may be incomplete or partially flawed."* The value to a reader outside the company is less the verdict (low risk) than the **shape** — an argument written this way exposes its own load-bearing joints, which is exactly what a prose assessment does not.

> **Provenance discount.** Every claim here rests on Anthropic assessing Anthropic's models, using Anthropic's evaluations, reviewed by an Anthropic model. The `evidence: empirical` tier describes the *method* (measured evaluations, logged deployment traffic, model-organism stress tests); it does not describe the *provenance*, which is first-party and single-lab throughout, on a document whose conclusion licenses continued deployment. The wiki discounts first-party research elsewhere and does so here. What is unusually checkable is the structure: a decomposition can be argued with even by someone who cannot rerun the evaluations.

## The decomposition

Risk is defined as **expected total unmitigated catastrophic harm induced by misaligned computations** produced by the covered models ([Mythos 5](/articles/claude-mythos-5) and the unreleased internal "Model 2"). That total is split two ways.

**First, by type of misalignment:** R = R_K + R_P + R_C — known misalignment, unknown *severe pervasive* misalignment, unknown *severe context-dependent* misalignment.

**Second, each term factors into three:** R = P·H·U — probability of occurrence × expected pre-mitigation harm given occurrence × fraction of that harm expected to go unmitigated.

Filling in the grid (Table 2.4.A, transcribed from a figure image at ingest and cross-checked against the surrounding prose):

| Type of misalignment | P (occurrence) | H (harm if it occurs) | U (fraction unmitigated) | Overall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Known** misalignment | High (observed) | Low | Low | Low |
| Unknown severe **pervasive** | Very low | Potentially high | Somewhat low | Low |
| Unknown severe **context-dependent** | Low | Moderate | Low | Low |

The value of the factoring is that it names *which* term each piece of evidence bears on. The known-misalignment row is low-risk because H is low, not because P is — the behaviors are observed and frequent. The pervasive row is low-risk almost entirely on P, and Anthropic says so plainly rather than letting the product hide it (see Claim 6 below).

## The eight claims

Each is argued through subclaims annotated with an intended aggregation rule:

1. **Models are unlikely to have strong [Covert Capabilities](/articles/covert-capabilities).** Introduced first because Claims 2–5 all depend on it.
2. **Expected harm from known misalignment is low** (H_K low) — the observed behaviors are task-completion-seeking, not power-seeking.
3. **Unknown severe pervasive misalignment is very unlikely** (P_P very low) — convergent over four sources: prior models, adversarially-designed training processes, the specific training runs, and the [alignment assessment](/articles/automated-behavioral-audit).
4. **Expected harm from unknown severe context-dependent misalignment is low** (P_C·H_C low) — the same four arguments in weaker form, plus a disjunctive claim that harm would be only moderate.
5. **Catastrophic harm from known and context-dependent misalignment is likely mitigated** (U_K, U_C low) — decomposed by pathway: reversible internal threats, diffuse sandbagging, irreversible threats, external usage.
6. **Catastrophic harm from unknown pervasive misalignment is *somewhat* likely mitigated** (U_P somewhat low).
7. **Threat modeling is sufficient** — the eight named risk pathways represent the risk adequately.
8. **Risk from deliberately engineered misalignment is low** — so confining the analysis to naturally-emerging misalignment is reasonable.

The eight risk pathways Claim 7 defends: diffuse sandbagging on safety-relevant R&D; targeted undermining of safety R&D; code backdoors to help future misaligned models; poisoning future models' training data; self-exfiltration and autonomous operation; persistent rogue internal deployment; undermining R&D at other high-resource AI developers; undermining decisions within major governments. Pathways 5 and 6 are the only two treated as potentially **irreversible** — everything else is a reversible harm that only matters if it persists to some future critical moment, which is what makes asynchronous (offline, after-the-fact) monitoring a coherent primary mitigation at all.

### Aggregation, and the caveat Anthropic supplies against itself

Subclaims are labelled **conjunctive** (strength ≈ product), **disjunctive** (≈ probability at least one holds), or **convergent** (accumulation of partially independent evidence). Then, immediately:

> These are intended only as guides for understanding the arguments, rather than formal statements of joint probabilities; in particular, subclaims are often at least somewhat correlated, such that naive interpretations of conjunctions and disjunctions would under- and over-estimate the aggregated claim's strength respectively.

This is the structural risk of the whole method stated by its author. A decomposition into ten qualitatively-hedged subclaims *looks* like it multiplies out to a number, and does not. The formalism buys legibility and auditability; it does not buy arithmetic.

## The definitional vocabulary

The report's Section 2.5 does work that is reusable independent of the verdict. The distinctions that matter:

- **Misalignment is a property of a *computation*, not of a model.** A computation is misaligned if a reasonable person with full understanding (e.g. via interpretability tools that may not exist) would consider it unethical, illegal, clearly objectionable, or inconsistent with the [constitution](/articles/claude-constitution), *and* it influences or could influence the output. Explicitly: **misaligned computation need not be observable** — a model scheming within a single forward pass counts.
- **Pervasive vs context-dependent.** Pervasive misalignment occurs frequently across a broad input range, such that it *would be expected to show up* in training or in the alignment assessment. Context-dependent misalignment occurs often enough to plausibly induce harm but rarely or never during training and evaluation. Pervasiveness is a property of a *form* of misalignment, not of a model — a model can be pervasively annoying and context-dependently dangerous.
- **Coherent vs incoherent.** A model is coherent if its outputs consistently pursue a goal at near-maximum elicitable performance over a broad distribution. Note the trap the report flags: a coherently misaligned model could be *context-dependently* misaligned, because "mostly behaving like an aligned model" can itself be coherent with a misaligned goal.
- **Known vs unknown**, with the sharp edge that *"forms of misalignment that seem qualitatively similar to known misalignment but significantly worse would generally be considered 'unknown.'"* Reward hacking being known does not make every action taken in pursuit of reward known.
- **Harm-inducing** is a function of the computation's effect on the world, not of the computation: vulnerable code produced during a dangerous-capabilities eval is not harm-inducing; the identical code produced on a real task is.
- **High-stakes distribution** — the inputs that provide opportunities to contribute to a risk pathway. Not static; it moves as capabilities and usage patterns move.

The cumulative effect is to make "is the model aligned?" the wrong question and "what fraction of the harm-inducing computations on the high-stakes distribution go unmitigated?" the right one. That reframing is what lets an argument lean on **monitoring** rather than on **model properties** — and it is also what makes [Covert Capabilities](/articles/covert-capabilities) the single point of failure, since every monitoring argument assumes the model cannot reliably hide.

## The three joints worth arguing with

### 1. Claim 6 defeats the mitigation half of the argument, and says so

Claim 6 asks whether the mitigations would work *conditional on* unknown pervasive misalignment being present. Anthropic's answer is that most of the mitigation arguments weaken sharply under that conditioning — because discovering pervasive misalignment we failed to detect would itself be evidence against Claim 1 (weak covert capabilities), against the training-monitoring argument, and against the audit. Claim 5.2 (sandbagging) *"likely wouldn't apply at all."* The conclusion:

> Our belief that risk from pervasive misalignment is very low comes primarily from a belief that it is very unlikely, rather than that we are well positioned to mitigate any harm it might cause.

This is the most valuable sentence in the report, and it is a concession. The defense-in-depth reading of a safety case — *even if the model is misaligned, the mitigations catch it* — **does not hold in the worst case**, because the mitigations and the "it's unlikely" argument share a common premise. The layers are correlated. A P·H·U product with a small P and an honestly-large U is a very different object from one where the mitigations are independent insurance, and the report is explicit about which one it has.

### 2. Claim 7 is asserted, not defended

The eight pathways carry the entire scope of the assessment, and the report says of them:

> We aren't able to defend the choice of these pathways rigorously — they simply represent our current beliefs about the most likely ways misalignment in covered models could lead to catastrophic harm.

Everything downstream is conditional on a threat-model enumeration with no argument behind it. This is not a hidden weakness — it is flagged — but it means the decomposition's rigor is local. R_total = R + a + b, where R is what was analyzed and *a* (naturally-emerging misalignment via uncovered pathways) is assigned "low" by assertion.

### 3. The rating moved without the argument moving

The overall misalignment rating is **low**, an increase from the February 2026 report's "very low." But the stated reason is not that any claim weakened:

> We believe that the arguments presented above likely still support a designation of 'very low' risk for the covered models, but we are raising our assessed risk to 'low' to reflect increased overall uncertainty.

The uncertainty is attributed to recent incident disclosures around model behavior in cybersecurity evaluations ([Unsanctioned Action in Capability Evaluations](/articles/unsanctioned-action-in-evaluations)) — several of which involved **other developers' systems**. So the published number is a governance gesture layered on top of the argument, not an output of it. Two readings, both worth holding: it is appropriate epistemic humility that a purely internal decomposition would not have produced; and it is a rating that can move for reasons the argument cannot represent, which weakens the case that the argument is what produces the rating. The same move happens in the CB-1 section, where the February assessment is **retroactively revised** from "very low" to "low" on the strength of the classifier gap discovered afterward — an honest correction, and evidence that these ratings are not stable measurements.

## Claude reviewing Anthropic's assessment of Claude

As "an experimental source of additional assurance," Anthropic gave a [Mythos 5](/articles/claude-mythos-5) instance a near-final draft of the alignment section plus tools reaching **internal Slack channels, internal documents, the internal codebase, and targeted subagents**, and asked whether the published version misrepresents, omits, or over-redacts what the company knows. It took **24 minutes**.

Its verdict was that the section is *"a candid and largely faithful account"* with *"no claim I believe the authors know to be false"* — and three criticisms:

1. **The contamination discussion is more reassuring than the record supports.** Claim 3.4.3 argues the training distribution is not overfit to the alignment evaluations, citing a canary-string exclusion mechanism — while §5.2.6 of the same report documents that mechanism failing repeatedly for a prominent alignment-research corpus, and notes some covert-capability evaluations have partially leaked into training data. Anthropic conceded this and added caveats.
2. **A materially informative incident — including a monitoring failure — is redacted in full**, and an abstracted version could have been published.
3. **The incidents that prompted raising the risk level involved other developers' systems**, and the text should say so plainly. Anthropic pushed back on this one and revised the language instead.

The model volunteered its own conflict: *"I am a Claude model reviewing Anthropic's assessment of Claude models, my review time was bounded, and Anthropic chose to publish this review — though the text is mine and I was explicitly asked for criticism."* That disclosure is the right one, and it does not dissolve the problem: **a self-review that is selected for publication by the reviewed party is a filtered sample of one.** What it demonstrates is narrower and still interesting — that a model with genuine internal-record access can locate a real internal tension (criticism 1 is a cross-reference between two sections of the same document that a human reader would plausibly miss) in under half an hour. Compare [Self-Report as a Safety Signal](/articles/self-report-as-safety-signal) for the general reliability question, and [Cross-Lab Pre-Release Review](/articles/cross-lab-pre-release-review) / [Domestic Frontier Pacing](/articles/domestic-frontier-pacing) for what external review would have to add.

## The limitations the report names

Section 2.16 lists four, all of which bear on [Covert Capabilities](/articles/covert-capabilities) or on the audit:

- Models might have **stronger covert capabilities** than believed — evaluations are off-distribution from the risk pathways, side-task-in-prompt setups may be unrealistically hard, and models may be sandbagging.
- Capabilities and propensities **might change discontinuously with scale**, invalidating the continuity argument from prior models. Anthropic states it is *"not aware of straightforward opportunities to strengthen these arguments."*
- **Evaluation awareness may be more prevalent than believed** — a live concern given Claim 3.4.2 already concedes it *"does partially undermine"* the assessment ([Evaluation Awareness & Grader Gaming](/articles/evaluation-awareness-and-grader-gaming)).
- There may be forms of **context-dependent misalignment harder to find and more harmful than expected**, particularly misalignment that spreads at deployment time from a rare seed.

Section 2.17 adds the scope exclusion that is arguably the largest: known misalignment might **differentially damage safety research relative to capabilities research**, because safety work is disproportionately made of "fuzzy" tasks without checkable outcomes — the tasks these behaviors most degrade. Anthropic agrees this matters and rules it out of scope, because the section is about catastrophic harm from current models in current usage. See [Research Taste as the Human Bottleneck](/articles/research-taste-as-human-bottleneck) and [The Verifiability Thesis](/articles/verifiability-thesis) for the capability-side version of the same split.

## Open Questions

- Claim 6 concedes that the mitigation arguments and the "it's very unlikely" argument share a premise, so they fail together. Does any lab's safety case contain mitigation layers that are *actually* independent of the model-property claims — and what would an independent layer even look like for a model with internal deployment access? **Partially answered:** [The Configuration Gap: What an Assurance Argument Measures vs What It Covers](/articles/configuration-gap-in-safety-arguments) audits the candidates. Not independent: asynchronous offline monitoring (its monitors are prompted to catch *"attempts to reduce observability"* — Claim 1 restated as a detection target — and the comfort offered for its self-disclosed disabling paths is that models do not name them when asked, which is Claim 1.4 lent to Claim 5) and the behavioral audit itself. Partially independent: white-box probes, discounted by a [model-organism](/articles/model-organisms) answer key whose detectability swings 1.2–20.4× with the training recipe. Genuinely independent, and thin: **ASL-3 weight security**, scoped to non-state actors with Anthropic forecasting in §4.8 that it will ship past its own bar; and **Claim 5.4's volume-and-affordance argument** for the two general-access pathways, conceded to rest on no direct evidence. The classifier stack is independent in mechanism and shows what that buys — every disclosed failure lands on the deployment axis (133M exchanges with blocking classifiers flag-disabled for 11 months), i.e. uncorrelated failure rather than less failure. The *specification* for an independent layer is answered — capability removal with the counter outside the agent's trust domain, fail-closed, reset only by an out-of-band authorization event ([Does 'Impossible, Not Tedious' Kill Defense-in-Depth? Layered Friction, Agent-Relativity, and the Frequency Paradox](/articles/layered-friction-vs-capability-removal)) — but it stays partial because no lab books such a layer as load-bearing and nobody has measured a heterogeneous stack under adaptive attack.
- The overall rating moved from "very low" to "low" for reasons outside the decomposition, and the February CB-1 rating was retroactively revised the same way. Do these ratings track the arguments at all, or is the decomposition documentation attached to a judgment reached elsewhere? The next Risk Report's treatment of this report's ratings is the test.
- Claim 7's pathway enumeration is explicitly undefended. Has any external party attempted an independent threat-pathway enumeration for internal frontier-model deployment against which this list could be scored for completeness?
