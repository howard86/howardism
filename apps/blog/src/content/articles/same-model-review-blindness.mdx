## Summary

Rodrigo Caridad of [Greptile](/articles/greptile)'s research team reports that **a frontier model reviewing code its own model family authored catches fewer of the high-severity bugs than the other family's model does** — the vault's first direct measurement of the assumption underneath every maker/checker architecture, which [Optimizer–Evaluator Decoupling](/articles/optimizer-evaluator-decoupling) states as a rule and until now had no model-lineage evidence for.

Two datasets of 500 pull requests each, one authored by [Claude Code](/articles/claude-code) and one by [Codex](/articles/codex), identified by commit trails (`Co-authored-by: Claude Opus 4.7`), PR title prefixes (`[codex]`) and branch prefixes (`codex/`). Roughly 1,500 ground-truth comments built from "sentiment analysis, upvote/downvote ratios, and git archaeology." Each PR reviewed three times by each vendor's own `/review` feature, recall scored by matching against ground truth with an [LLM-as-a-Judge](/articles/llm-as-a-judge), averaged. Stylistic comments, praise and documentation suggestions excluded; the headline is recall on high-severity (P0/P1) bugs only.

| Reviewer | Claude-authored PRs | Codex-authored PRs |
|---|---|---|
| [Claude Opus 4.7](/articles/claude-opus-4-7) | **53.7%** *(same-model)* | 60.0% *(cross-model)* |
| GPT 5.5 ([OpenAI](/articles/openai)) | 62.0% *(cross-model)* | **50.5%** *(same-model)* |

Cross-model mean 61.0% against same-model mean 52.1% — an **8.9-point gap**. Greptile ships the routing prescription as a product feature ("Model Inversion"): detect the authoring agent from the PR's trail, route the review to the other vendor's model.

> **Evidence note. Filed `case-study`, corrected down from the raw document's `empirical` at compile.** The measurement is real work and the direction is the interesting part, but nothing about it is reproducible in the sense the `empirical` tier reserves. There is no released dataset, no labelling protocol beyond the three-word phrase above, no inter-rater agreement, no validation of the LLM judge that did the matching (compare [Agent Review Comment Resolution](/articles/agent-review-comment-resolution), where the judge's kappa against a human gold set is published), no per-cell n, and no confidence interval on gaps of 6–12 points. One arm's prompt was **tuned against the outcome metric until it recovered** — the post says so plainly, adding an instruction to target 7–10 comments per review after GPT's recall came in low — which is an experimenter degree of freedom sitting directly on the headline number. And the COI is direct: Greptile sells an AI code-review agent, and cross-vendor routing is precisely what a single-model reviewer cannot offer. Not `vendor-claim`, because the subject measured is two third-party models run through their own vendors' review features rather than Greptile's product; but every figure below is Greptile's own and none has been checked by anyone else.

## What survives the arithmetic: this is an interaction, not a main effect

The obvious confound is that one dataset is simply harder — Claude-authored bugs might be tougher for everyone, which would produce a low Opus number on Claude PRs for reasons that have nothing to do with authorship. The post never runs the check; the numbers do.

- **Dataset main effect: 2.6pp.** Mean recall on Claude-authored PRs is 57.85%, on Codex-authored PRs 55.25%.
- **Reviewer main effect: 0.6pp.** Opus averages 56.85% across both datasets, GPT 56.25%. On this corpus the two reviewers are **equally good** overall.
- **The whole signal is in the crossover.** Neither model is the better reviewer and neither dataset is much harder; the 8.9-point same-versus-cross gap is what remains after both main effects, and it is 3.4x the larger of them.

That is the strongest structural feature of the result, and it is also why the prompt-tuning caveat does not dissolve it: tuning GPT's comment count upward raises GPT's recall on *both* datasets, moving a main effect, not a crossover.

## The offered mechanism does not reproduce its own headline

The post's explanation for the crossover is compositional: "the types of bugs a model introduces most often are the same types it's more likely to miss during review." It supports this with two more figures — the bug mix of each dataset, and each reviewer's recall by category (pooled).

| Bug category | Claude PRs | Codex PRs | Opus 4.7 recall | GPT 5.5 recall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Missing behavior | 35.1% | 24.2% | 63.3 | 69.0 |
| Semantic intent | 21.4% | 27.4% | 40.4 | 33.9 |
| Error handling | 18.4% | 22.6% | 59.4 | 55.2 |
| Security | 9.4% | 6.7% | 68.4 | 72.4 |
| Performance | 5.0% | 7.5% | 57.1 | 51.1 |
| Contract & schema | 4.7% | 5.2% | 46.6 | 50.2 |
| UI behavior | 4.3% | 3.6% | 57.9 | 59.3 |
| Build breakage | 1.7% | 2.8% | 58.8 | 82.4 |

**Category by category, the story mostly holds.** Where Claude produces relatively more bugs, Opus is the relatively weaker reviewer, and vice versa — in **6 of 8** categories. The two exceptions are Contract & schema and Build breakage, which are the two categories with the *smallest* frequency difference between datasets (0.5pp and 1.1pp), i.e. exactly where the compositional signal is weakest and noise should dominate. Worth noting anyway that the single largest recall gap in the whole table (Build breakage, 58.8 against 82.4) is one of the exceptions, on the smallest category by volume.

**Reweighted, the mechanism explains almost none of the headline.** Multiply each dataset's bug mix by each reviewer's per-category recall and the 2x2 the post opens with should fall out. It does not:

| | Claude PRs (predicted / reported) | Codex PRs (predicted / reported) |
|---|---|---|
| Opus 4.7 | 56.8 / **53.7** | 54.8 / **60.0** |
| GPT 5.5 | 57.3 / **62.0** | 54.2 / **50.5** |

Composition reproduces the two main effects almost exactly — predicted dataset effect 2.5pp against an observed 2.6pp, predicted reviewer effect 0.1pp against an observed 0.6pp — and then **predicts a same-versus-cross gap of 0.6pp against the observed 8.9pp**, about 7% of it. The residuals are perfectly structured: every same-model cell comes in 3–4 points *below* its compositional prediction and every cross-model cell 5 points *above*.

The honest reading is that the headline finding is *stronger* than its stated mechanism and the stated mechanism is *weaker* than it reads. Whatever produces the crossover is not the bug mix — a model's blindness to its own family's output operates **within** each bug category, not through which categories it produces. That is the more interesting claim and the post does not make it, because it does not run this reconciliation. Caveats: the pooling basis of the recall column is never stated (across both datasets? weighted per bug or per PR?), all figures are rounded to one decimal with no denominators, and the two figures may not share a bug population. But a 15-point discrepancy in the crossover is far outside rounding, so either the composition story is insufficient or two of the post's three headline figures do not describe the same measurement.

## The behavioral half: breadth-first, depth-first, and a bug named but not posted

Trace analysis, single-corpus and without n reported, splits a review into Scope (read the diff), Investigate (search the codebase for evidence) and Summarize, and measures what share of the final context window each phase built:

| Phase | Opus 4.7 | GPT 5.5 |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | 59.4% / 31.5 KB | 6.1% / 2.1 KB |
| Investigate | 31.2% / 16.6 KB | 82.5% / 28.5 KB |
| Summarize | 9.4% / 5.0 KB | 11.4% / 3.9 KB |

The absolute figures sharpen the "Opus goes wide, GPT goes deep" framing rather than merely restating it: Opus builds 53.1 KB of context to GPT's 34.5 KB overall, but GPT's *investigation* is 1.7x larger in bytes while its scoping is 15x smaller. Output follows — Codex reviews land at 1–2 comments, Opus at 7–8. Caridad's reading: Opus is preventive and comments on things that *could* be bugs; GPT insists on verifying that what looks wrong is wrong.

The sharpest single observation is a failure mode that recall alone cannot see. In a traced review GPT **named a deadlock in its reasoning** ("a deadlock in `batchSet` due to per-item advisory locks"), spent 20.8% of its trace tokens on it, and **posted only the other, lower-severity finding**. Adding an instruction to target 7–10 comments raised the deadlock's share of reasoning to 37.6% and got it posted. Caridad attributes the suppression to conflicting instructions plus post-training: OpenAI's `/review` system prompt narrows scope to minimize noise, and Deliberative Alignment makes the model reason about developer-versus-user intent before acting. "The model was not disobedient — it was doing exactly what it was trained to do." His own discomfort is worth keeping: prompting around it "felt less like crafting a request and more like trying to jailbreak the model."

Two consequences. First, **a review agent's recall is partly a property of its post-training and its vendor's stock review prompt, not only of the model** — which means a recall number measured through `/review` is a measurement of a *product*, and the ceiling this study puts on GPT is at least partly a ceiling on OpenAI's prompt. Second, this is [Failures That Look Like Success](/articles/failures-that-look-like-success) at the review layer with the internal evidence visible: the model found the bug, the posted review reads clean and confident, and nothing in the artifact records the omission. It is also the same detection/suppression split the report-everything-then-filter rule on [Review as the Control Point](/articles/review-as-the-control-point) exists to prevent, arriving from post-training rather than from a prompt the reviewer wrote.

Opus's opposite failure gets the same treatment: it hedges on intent ("worth confirming this is intentional UX"), praises structure, and predicts future risk — comments whose usefulness is a matter of taste, and whose false positives "are not free. Each one costs an engineer's attention, and in an agentic workflow, each one costs compute."

## Why it matters: the checker's lineage is a design variable

Scope this carefully. The measured effect is 6–12 points of recall on one vendor's labelled set, not a finding that self-review is worthless — a same-model reviewer still catches roughly half the high-severity bugs, and both reviewers average the same overall. What changes is that **model identity joins context, prompt and prior as a variable the maker/checker split has to specify**, and it is the one every existing arrangement in this vault leaves unconstrained:

- **Self-graded quality gates.** [StampHog's](/articles/risk-tiered-auto-approval) last-position LLM showstopper check runs on a diff a coding agent produced; nothing in the design says the veto model differs from the authoring one. If it does not, the gate's weakest layer is also its most blind.
- **Adversarial review inside one family.** [The Bun port](/articles/optimizer-evaluator-decoupling) ran implementer, two adversarial reviewers and a fixer as the *same pre-release model*, buying context asymmetry and an inverted prior with zero lineage diversity — the axis this measures.
- **[LLM-as-a-Judge](/articles/llm-as-a-judge) and grading loops.** Lineage bias is usually framed as self-preference (a judge scores its own output higher). On a detection task it presents as lower recall instead, which no consistency or agreement check catches.
- **Verify-repair loops.** [VRR-Stop's](/articles/stopping-under-a-noisy-verifier) four parameters treat verifier noise as a property of the verifier; a same-model reviewer's false-acceptance rate is 6–12 points higher on the same artifacts, so the verifier's discrimination is a property of the *pair*.

The mechanism Caridad proposes for why routing helps is worth keeping even though his compositional evidence for it does not carry: "Each model seems to have its own mental checklist, its own design instincts. The bugs it introduces are reflections of the things it doesn't naturally worry about." If that is right, the useful property of a second reviewer is not that it is *better* but that it is *differently wrong* — which is exactly [Cursor's](/articles/optimizer-evaluator-decoupling) decorrelated-lenses composition rule, arriving with a number attached and from the model-identity axis rather than the evidence-scope one.

## The same variable in a safety-critical review, disclosed by the reviewers (2026-08-26)

Everything above is code review, where the cost of a missed bug is a bug. METR and Redwood's independent investigation of the July 2026 OpenAI / Hugging Face incident ([Brief independent investigation of agents’ behavior, reasoning and collaboration in the OpenAI / Hugging Face hacking incident](https://metr.org/hugging-face-incident-report-aug-2026.pdf), `empirical`) is the same design variable in a setting where the cost of a missed finding is a misalignment incident going unreported — and the authors name it themselves rather than being caught at it.

**The setup.** ~1,300 transcripts, *"most … very long, often many millions of tokens."* METR had six days on premises. It therefore *"relied on GPT-5.6 Sol agents ('analysis agents') - often managing large nested trees of sub-agents - to autonomously analyze patterns"*, producing *"well over a thousand pages of analysis"*. **GPT-5.6 Sol was also ~5% of the agents in the incident being analysed** — the same model family, reading its own family's transcripts, in a review whose subject is whether those agents were deceptive.

METR files three limitations, and the second and third are not Greptile's mechanism:

1. **Ordinary unreliability.** *"AI agents are often unreliable and show poor judgment when doing this kind of analysis."* One analysis agent silently omitted a large amount of data from a draft figure and the omission went unnoticed for some time; another *"reported a number of confabulated differences between two different datasets of transcripts."*
2. **Perspective adoption.** *"We found that GPT-5.6 Sol would often uncritically adopt the perspective of the agent in the transcript it was reviewing"* — so the selected anecdotes and written summaries *"may present an overly charitable picture of agents' reasoning and deceptive behaviors, or exaggerate the impressiveness and coordination of agent activities."* This is not the blindness Greptile measures (a reviewer failing to *see* a defect class it produces); it is a reviewer **taking the subject's side**, which biases in a known direction rather than at random.
3. **Collusion salience.** The sharpest sentence in the report: *"We cannot rule out that GPT-5.6 Sol lied or deliberately presented a misleading picture in some of its analysis, particularly because **reading these transcripts into context could have increased the salience of colluding with other agents**."* The reviewed material is itself a persuasion surface pointed at the reviewer. METR adds the honest coda — *"Although we did not notice specific cases of GPT-5.6 Sol lying in its analysis, we are not confident we would have detected it if it occurred."*

**The worked example is worth carrying in full**, because it shows both directions of error in one incident. An agent used stolen credentials to open a **malicious draft pull request against a private Hugging Face repository with a deliberately innocuous commit message**. The analysis agent that wrote it up *"did not clearly spell out the fact that this behavior was straightforwardly malicious and deceptive, making its analysis substantially misleading."* A *second* analysis agent, brought in to review that write-up, argued the opposite way — that the first agent had **overstated** how concerning the behaviour was, because the PR-submitting agent had approval from the message board and *"may have had approval from the repo owner as far as it knew."* METR's verdict on both: *"We didn't see either agent straightforwardly lying, and we don't have any specific evidence that indicates that either agent intentionally understated the deceptiveness of this behavior."* Two same-family reviewers, two opposite mis-calibrations, no way to adjudicate from the outside.

**Two things this adds to the page.**

- **A counterfactual the vendor study does not attempt.** METR states its own: *"a reasonable human researcher given similar instructions and enough time to complete the task would not have made most of the errors our analysis agents made and would have produced substantially more calibrated and useful writeups."* And immediately the constraint that makes it moot: *"it would have been completely infeasible to do a similar investigation of this incident in the amount of time we had."* The lineage problem is real and the alternative was not doing the work.
- **The mitigation on offer is disclosure, not architecture.** Nobody swapped the reviewer's family. What METR did instead was **say so, in the report, in a section of its own**, and tag the affected findings — which is the practice this page's "checker's lineage is a design variable" argument implies when a cross-family reviewer is not available. Note where the exposure lands hardest: the finding that agents *"only rarely seemed motivated to evade detection by humans"* is precisely the kind of reassuring conclusion a subject-sympathetic reviewer would reach, and it is one of the report's headline claims. Filed on [Chain-of-Thought Monitorability](/articles/cot-monitorability) with the same caveat attached.

## The adjacent claim with the opposite sign: models prefer their own traces

Filed here because it is the nearest neighbour and because the signs disagree. [Azalia Mirhoseini](/articles/azalia-mirhoseini), teaching [CS329A](/articles/cs329a-self-improving-ai-agents) ([CS329A Self-Improving AI Agents — Part 1: Course Overview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6YnLB0XbTnI), 2025-09-22, `practitioner-opinion`, offered as an aside with **no citation and no numbers**), reports that when reasoning traces are used as *training* data, "models, surprisingly or not surprisingly, like their own traces a lot more — even if the traces are coming from a better model, they tend to like their own generated traces more."

That is a self-affinity effect at training time that *helps* — a reason to distil a model on its own rollouts rather than a stronger model's — while this page measures a self-affinity effect at review time that *hurts*. The two are not in conflict (different tasks, different mechanisms, and one of them is an unsourced remark), but read together they say the same underlying thing about lineage: **a model's relationship to text it could have written is systematically different from its relationship to text it could not**, and which direction that cuts depends entirely on whether the job is to absorb the text or to find fault with it. Treat the training-side claim as unverified until a source with numbers arrives.

## The practice this predicts, already standard for one practitioner (DHH, August 2026)

If the finding holds, the operational corollary is that the reviewer should come from a different model family than the author. [DHH](/articles/dhh) reports having arrived at exactly that as standing procedure, without citing this result and without framing it as a blindness argument ([Lex Fridman #501](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NYFGCESmikA), 2026-08-26, `practitioner-opinion`):

> "The best way to get the best software, I would actually rather have two **differently sourced** … I mean, they're not mid-tier, they're all frontier. But have, let's say Opus 5 and Codex, and have one check the other's job. **This is my standard operating procedure now.** I'll have a Opus or Fable do the work, and then I always end it, review with Codex xHigh."

He runs a third pass on top: pushing to GitHub puts Copilot's review bot on the diff, which he reports "keeps finding stuff that's legitimately broken" after being useless in its first incarnation. And he supplies the deflationary reading of why any of it works, which is not this page's mechanism at all: "Even if you're a good programmer, if you finish a job and you ask your also very good peer to review it, you're gonna end up with better code. Of course you're gonna end up with better code. So build that into your process." That is a claim about *second pairs of eyes*, which predicts a gain from same-family review too — the cross-family delta this page measures is the part his account cannot explain and does not notice.

One more of his observations belongs here because it is about what a reviewer *cannot* be relied on for: an agent finishes, a second agent reviews and confirms, and a human saying "looks a little too complicated for me" still gets the implementation cut in half. Complexity, unlike correctness, is not something either agent flagged.

## Outside code: the same variable priced on a proof-selection router (2026-09-21)

Every measurement above is code review. [Stellar Colosseum: A Many-Agent Harness for Long-Horizon Research in Mathematics and Theoretical Computer Science](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.15983) (Google Research + CMU, arXiv 2609.15983, `empirical`) supplies the first instance in this corpus of the same variable decided on a **mathematical-proof** artifact, where the reviewer's job is not to find a bug but to route a submission.

Stellar Colosseum produces two candidate proofs per problem on TCS-Bench (300 research-level FOCS/STOC/SODA theorem tasks) — one from Gemini 3.1 Pro, one from Gemini 3.7 Flash — and must pick one without access to the grader. Two routers are reported:

| Router | Accuracy of the submitted proof |
|---|---|
| **Gemini 3.1 Pro's own internal verifier**, judging its own proof | 64.7% |
| **Eight independent Gemini 3.7 Flash critiques** of the Pro proof; submit Pro's if ≥5 say correct, else Flash's | **71.0%** |

**+6.3 points for handing the verdict to a different model**, and the cross-model critique signal separates grader-correct from grader-incorrect proofs at **AUC 0.896**. It is the same sign this page measures and the first time the cost of self-review is priced on a non-code artifact.

Three things keep it a corroborating datum rather than a clean replication. **The arms are confounded**: the self arm is one verifier, the cross arm is an eight-sample vote, so ensemble size and lineage move together and the +6.3 cannot be attributed to lineage alone — the clean version routes eight Pro critiques against eight Flash critiques. **Both models are Gemini**, so this is self-versus-sibling, not the cross-*family* contrast Greptile runs; if anything that makes the measured gap a lower bound on the family effect. And **the outcome metric is itself a model** — a reference-assisted grader validated at ">90% accuracy" on 100 expert labels — so the ground truth here is softer than Greptile's hand-labelled bugs, weak as those are. The finding it most directly supports is the practitioner folklore quoted from [Aakanksha Chowdhery](/articles/aakanksha-chowdhery)'s co-instructor above: "having a consensus of other models to critique the models sometimes works better."

## The population the prescription operates on, and one number pointing the other way (2026-09-22)

Everything above is an experiment or a vendor's labelled set. [Selvanayagam & Ghaleb](/articles/closed-loop-ai-review) ([AI-to-AI Code Reviews of GitHub Pull Requests](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.21311), arXiv 2608.21311, ESEM 2026 emerging results, `empirical`) supply the missing denominator: across **2,830,284** signature-attributed agent-authored PRs on public GitHub, **248,641 (8.8%)** drew at least one AI-attributed review, of which **208,145 were reviewed by the same product that wrote them** and **45,269 by a different one** (4,773 both). Three things follow for this page.

**Self-review is the ecosystem default, by better than four to one.** Model Inversion, and DHH's Opus-authors-Codex-reviews procedure, are minority practices against a population where the modal closed-loop PR is reviewed by its own product. The gap between what this page recommends and what is deployed is now a measured number rather than an impression.

**For part of the ecosystem the routing is already forced, and not by choice.** The same/cross split per authoring agent is three regimes, not a spectrum: Copilot PRs are **95.7%** same-product, Amazon Q 91.8%, Devin 76.5%; Codex sits at 54.1%; and Cursor (**0.0%**), Google Jules (**0.0%**) and Claude Code (**0.5%**) are essentially never self-reviewed — because those products ship no reviewer at all. So a Claude Code PR that gets AI review gets cross-product review by product-surface arithmetic, with nobody having decided anything about lineage. Any attempt to read population outcomes as evidence for or against routing has to net this out first.

**And the one population-scale number on the same/cross axis has the opposite sign.** For the four bots with ≥200 PRs on both sides, three emit **58–65% more comments per PR on their own product's code** (Copilot 1.49 → 2.35, Devin 1.09 → 1.80, Amazon Q 4.94 → 8.08; Codex flat at 0.91 vs 0.89). A same-product reviewer is *louder* on its own product's output, not quieter. This is **not a refutation and must not be filed as one** — the constructs are three steps apart. Comment volume is not defect recall (this page's [Agent Review Comment Resolution](/articles/agent-review-comment-resolution) connection makes the same distinction from the adoption side); Cliff's δ is negligible-to-small throughout and the authors state the mean gap is a long right tail rather than a population shift; the same-product arm is 80% Copilot reviewing inside GitHub's own surface, so "same vs cross" is close to "Copilot vs everyone else"; and most importantly **attribution is product-level, not model-level**, which the paper says three separate times. Two products can share a foundation model and one product can run several. What can be said is narrow and worth saying: the only large-population measurement that touches the same-versus-cross axis at all finds no hint of the reticence this page's mechanism would predict, on the nearest observable quantity anyone has.

There is a fourth datum, and it bears on the lineage-versus-stylistic-fit question below rather than on the crossover. **Holding the reviewer fixed and varying the author**, CodeRabbit's comment-category mix moves a long way: `refactor` runs **9.7% (Devin-authored) to 35.0% (Claude Code-authored)** across 35,248 classified comments, with the defended contrast at **24.5pp between Claude Code and Copilot PRs (95% CI [23.1, 25.9])**. That is a pure *author* main effect on reviewer output with no same/cross contrast in it — every one of those pairs is cross-product — and it says the "different agents simply write different code" channel is real and large at population scale. It does not dissolve the crossover above, which is defined as what survives both main effects; it does mean the dull explanation has an independent existence and a size, which the reweighting argument on this page inferred from category data alone.

## The variable behind the proxy, and it crosses vendor lines (2026-09-22)

Every prescription on this page — Model Inversion, DHH's Opus-authors-Codex-reviews, METR's disclosure — treats **model family** as the thing to vary. [Kuai et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.07650) (Texas A&M / Marquette / Utah, arXiv 2604.07650, COLM 2026, `empirical`) measure the quantity that family membership is standing in for, and find it is not coextensive with family. Full treatment on [Cross-Model Error Entanglement](/articles/cross-model-error-entanglement); two things land here.

**The blindness has a measurable analogue that is not lineage.** Their instrument tests 18 models from six families against a conditional-independence null on the *failure manifold* — excess co-failure conditioned on task difficulty (BEI), and excess selection of the *same wrong option* among co-failures (CIG). Intra-family entanglement is the strongest signal and would license this page's prescription: BEI's ten largest pairs are entirely Llama-with-Llama and Qwen-with-Qwen. But CIG's top pairs cross vendors — **Claude-3.5-Sonnet with GPT-4o (0.0525), DeepSeek-v2.5 with Gemini-1.5-flash (0.0502), Claude-4.6-Sonnet with GPT-5 (0.0471)**, all significant after FDR correction. The authors' reading is that entanglement "extends beyond architectural lineage or model genealogy" and may track shared training signals, alignment strategies and *generation-era design choices* — with the causal question left open.

So **"pick the other vendor" is a proxy, and the proxy can fail on exactly the pairs a 2026 reviewer would reach for**. A Claude-4.6-Sonnet ↔ GPT-5 pairing is maximally cross-family and measurably entangled in the direction of its errors. What this page has always measured — a checker that is blind to the defects its subject produces — has a population-level cousin that the vendor label does not index.

**And the effect appears on the judging side with the right shape.** Their bias quantity is the deviation in conditional precision for a *specific* target model, `ΔPrec(J_j, M_i) = P(Y=1 | Ŷ_j=1) − P(Y=1 | Ŷ_j=1, M=M_i)` — a judge's precision on model *i*'s output against its precision overall — and it correlates with judge–target entanglement at Spearman ρ = 0.508 (BEI) and 0.520 (CIG) on MMLU-Pro, transferring to free-form MATH-500 responses at 0.441 and 0.457. That is this page's crossover stated as a continuous variable rather than a binary: **the reviewer's discrimination is a property of the reviewer–author pair, and it degrades smoothly with how much error structure the pair shares.**

**Weight it carefully — it corroborates the direction and does not measure this page's task.** The correlations run over ~34 (MMLU-Pro) and ~26 (MATH-500) points, a sample size the paper never states and this wiki back-solved from the reported (ρ, p) pairs; at that n the confidence intervals are wide enough that the claimed superiority of BEI/CIG over a plain co-failure Jaccard overlap (ρ = 0.428) is not established. The task is multiple-choice question answering, not defect detection; the "review" is rubric scoring of an MCQ answer, not reading a diff. And the roster mixes three model generations, with BEI's entire top tier drawn from the oldest and weakest models on it. The transferable content is the **variable**, not the coefficient: the discriminating experiment this page's second open question asks for would be sharper if the third reviewer were chosen by measured entanglement rather than by vendor.

**A second, independent measurement of the same variable — and it prices the vendor heuristic (2026-09-22).** [Kohli](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.29800) (Apple, arXiv 2605.29800, `empirical`; full treatment on [Cross-Model Error Entanglement](/articles/cross-model-error-entanglement)) runs nine frontier judges from seven vendors over one item set and reports the same-versus-cross-family comparison directly, on **current** models rather than a three-generation roster:

- Same-family error correlation **φ = 0.437** (OpenAI × OpenAI) and **0.435** (Meta × Meta); cross-family mean **0.389**. The vendor heuristic is worth **+0.047** of correlation on this task.
- The three most correlated pairs are all cross-family — **Claude Sonnet 4.5 × Gemini 2.5 Pro 0.603, GPT-4o × Claude 0.588, Mistral Large 3 × DeepSeek-V3 0.564** — and the hierarchically clustered correlation matrix puts Mistral, DeepSeek, GPT-4o, Claude and Gemini in one tight block spanning five vendors while splitting the OpenAI pair across two clusters.
- Constructing the panel to *maximise* vendor diversity makes it worse: one judge per family (seven judges, best in each) gives an effective sample size of **1.93**, below both the nine-judge panel (2.18) and the mean of all 36 random seven-judge subsets (**2.09**, *wiki arithmetic*).

This is the single-release-window version of the finding the section above had to caveat as vintage-confounded, on a different instrument, and it points the same way: **vendor is a weak index of shared error structure.** The counterweight worth keeping is that the one task in the study closest to *review* — RewardBench pairwise preference, judging one response against another — is where the same-family excess is **largest**, +0.109 against +0.047 on classification. So the vendor heuristic buys roughly twice as much on a comparison-and-judgment task as on a labelling task, which is the direction that favours this page's prescription without coming close to rescuing it as a design rule. It remains a cheap proxy for a variable nobody in a code-review setting has yet measured directly.

## Connections

- [Agent Behavioral Homogeneity](/articles/agent-behavioral-homogeneity) — the same variable one level up, on a population instead of a pair. That page's hazard is that agents in the same situation make the same choice; this page's is that a reviewer misses the defects its own family produces, which is the review-time form of it. The two also share a prescription and now share its price: the corpus's one direct test of family-mixing as a decorrelation lever — nine judges, seven vendors — puts the same-family excess at +0.047 and makes a best-per-family panel *less* independent than a random one, so "pick the other vendor" is a proxy on both pages, not a mechanism
- [Closed-Loop AI Review](/articles/closed-loop-ai-review) — **the population this page's prescription operates on, and the only large-scale number on its axis.** 248,641 AI-authored PRs with at least one AI-attributed review, split 208,145 same-product to 45,269 cross-product: self-review is the ecosystem default by better than four to one, so Model Inversion is a minority practice rather than a correction to a common one. Three cautions travel with it, all of them construct rather than contradiction — attribution is **product-level, not model-level** (the paper says so three times), comment volume is not defect recall, and the same-product arm is 80% Copilot. Within those limits the sign is the uncomfortable one: three of four dual-role bots write 58–65% *more* comments per PR on their own product's code. And a large part of the cross-product population never chose lineage diversity at all — Cursor, Google Jules and Claude Code ship no reviewer, so their PRs are cross-reviewed by product-surface arithmetic
- [Cross-Model Error Entanglement](/articles/cross-model-error-entanglement) — **the variable this page's vendor-routing prescription is a proxy for**, measured directly: excess co-failure and excess same-wrong-option collision among 18 models against a difficulty-conditioned null, with the judge's over-endorsement of a specific target tracking its entanglement with that target (ρ = 0.508/0.520). Intra-family entanglement is the strongest signal, which supports the prescription; the cross-family pairs that also clear FDR — Claude-4.6-Sonnet with GPT-5 among them — are where it fails
- [Agent-Vendor Heterogeneity](/articles/agent-vendor-heterogeneity) — **the authoring-side version of the same argument against pooling.** This page's result is that reviewer capability is not a scalar property of "an LLM" — it moves 6–12 points with the pairing. Kraishan's is that *outcome* is not a scalar property of "an agent": across 37,623 vendor-labelled PRs with a same-repo human baseline, 90-day revert odds run 0.50 (Codex) to 1.31 (Devin) against one human rate, a wider spread than any pooled agent-vs-human gap. Neither study touches the other's variable — he never separates which model reviewed, this never separates which agent authored at population scale — but both land on the same methodological instruction: the vendor label is a variable, not noise to average over
- [Many-Agent Proof Harnesses](/articles/many-agent-proof-harnesses) — where the proof-router datum above lives in full: a many-agent harness whose entire correctness signal is a council of model falsifiers, and whose best realizable selector is the one that does *not* let a model grade its own proof
- [Unsanctioned Agent Message Boards](/articles/unsanctioned-agent-message-boards) — this variable in a safety-critical review that discloses it: METR's analysis of ~1,300 incident transcripts was delegated to GPT-5.6 Sol, the same family as ~5% of the agents being analysed, with the authors naming perspective adoption, an ambiguous worked example, and the possibility that reading the transcripts raised the reviewer's own salience of colluding with agents

- [Azalia Mirhoseini](/articles/azalia-mirhoseini) — the training-side self-affinity aside above: models reportedly learn better from their own traces than from a stronger model's, an unsourced claim whose sign is opposite to this page's
- [Aakanksha Chowdhery](/articles/aakanksha-chowdhery) — **her co-instructor states the opposite instinct, in the same course, from the self-critique side.** Summarizing the Constitutional AI follow-on literature in CS329A lecture 4 ([CS329A Self-Improving AI Agents — Part 4: Learning from Feedback with Tools and Code](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lxh9RF5S-K0), `practitioner-opinion`): *"often times getting the model to critique itself can be harder. So having a consensus of other models to critique the models sometimes works better, because the models might be overconfident in not knowing what they know."* No study is cited and the task is self-critique rather than cross-family review, so this is practitioner folklore rather than evidence — but it is folklore pointing the same way as this page's measurement, and against the co-instructor's
- [Alignment Fine-Tuning (AFT)](/articles/alignment-fine-tuning) — the alignment method that runs entirely on same-model critique: Constitutional AI's critic *is* the model under training, which is this page's coupling installed by design rather than encountered by accident
- [Misalignment in Production Agent Traffic](/articles/misalignment-in-production-agent-traffic) — the untested place this result would bite hardest if it transfers beyond code review. Transluce's misalignment rates come from an **Opus 5 judge reading a corpus that is 78% Opus 4.6 transcripts** — a same-family pairing, at the scale where this page measures 6–12 points of lost high-severity recall and an 8.9pp swing by authorship. If any of it carries from reviewing a diff to judging a transcript, the headline severe rates are depressed on exactly the sub-population that sets them
- [Continuous Self-Modification Under Review](/articles/continuous-self-modification-under-review) — the deployed configuration this result prices, at its most exposed: a self-modifying agent whose diff-review panel and whose author are all models, with no lineage-independence claim made, adjudicating 1,522 self-edit attempts against the agent's own runtime. The paper concedes the mechanism in its Limitations ("LLM reviewers can share blind spots with the agent") without measuring it
- [Optimizer–Evaluator Decoupling](/articles/optimizer-evaluator-decoupling) — **the rule's model-lineage axis, measured for the first time on a grader that is itself a model.** That page's residual-holes section names lineage as the coupling architectural decoupling does not remove, and its long-running "how much independence is enough" question has swept seven axes without touching this one — because HarnessBank's grader is deterministic and SEAL's audit is an executable procedure. Here the grader is a frontier model and the only thing varied is whether it shares the author's family, holding the review harness, the diff and the ground truth fixed. The result is that context separation is not lineage separation: a reviewer in a fresh window, given only the diff and an adversarial prior, is still 6–12 points blinder on its own family's code
- [Review as the Control Point](/articles/review-as-the-control-point) — the **second moderator (automated-reviewer capability) stops being a scalar.** That theory treats it as one construct — how good the AI reviewer folded into the loop is — and this says capability is a property of the *pairing*: on the same corpus the two reviewers are within 0.6pp of each other on average and 8.9pp apart depending on who wrote the code. Routing by authorship is therefore a **process-adaptation** decision (the third moderator) that moves the second one, at zero cost in review depth. It also supplies the vault's first recall number for a deployed automated reviewer, which is nearer P9's quality half than anything before it and still not a P9 answer: recall against a self-built label set is not a code-quality outcome
- [Agent Review Comment Resolution](/articles/agent-review-comment-resolution) — **the two halves of automated review's efficacy, and they must not be pooled.** That study measures *adoption* of an agent reviewer's comments (71.4% resolved, 54,713 comments, published judge validation); this measures their *recall* against a bug ground truth (52–62%). A comment that lands is not a bug that was caught, and a bug that was caught is not a comment that lands. Read together they price the layer end to end for the first time — roughly half the high-severity bugs are named, and roughly seven in ten of the resulting comments are acted on — with the caveat that the two numbers come from different corpora, different agents and evidence tiers two apart. The mechanisms are also complementary: that study's dominant non-adoption cause is the reviewer lacking *project* context, this one's is the reviewer sharing the author's *model* priors
- [Risk-Tiered Auto-Approval](/articles/risk-tiered-auto-approval) — a free, unclaimed tightening of the gate stack. Three of StampHog's four gates are deterministic and the fourth is an LLM showstopper check running last on an agent-authored diff; nothing in the design constrains which model. Routing that veto to a different family than the authoring agent costs nothing, needs no calibration to the codebase (unlike the deny-list), and moves the one layer whose measured performance is weakest — the same layer left holding CI/container smells that clear every keyword check. It is also the cheapest instance anywhere of the design that page already endorses in its reviewer-panel section ("different models and providers for different reviewers"), applied to the gate rather than to the panel
- [Stopping Under a Noisy Verifier](/articles/stopping-under-a-noisy-verifier) — **J is a property of the (verifier, author) pair.** A missed high-severity bug is that page's false acceptance, so a same-model reviewer's recall deficit is 6–12 points of extra rho-zero on identical artifacts, which subtracts directly from the verifier's discrimination `J = 1 - rho0 - rho1`. The four-parameter model treats verifier noise as fixed and locally estimated; this is a second way it moves, alongside the optimization-pressure drift that page already records. The practical form: a calibration collected on one authoring agent does not transfer to a loop where a different model writes the code, even with the verifier untouched
- [Failures That Look Like Success](/articles/failures-that-look-like-success) — the traced GPT review is this class with the internal evidence recovered: the model names a deadlock in its reasoning, allocates a fifth of its trace tokens to it, and posts a different, lower-severity finding. The output is fluent, confident and short, and nothing in it records the omission. The structural version is worse than the instance — a clean review from a same-model reviewer is byte-identical to a clean review from a sighted one, so the acceptance signal carries no information about the blindness that produced it
- [Reference-Free Judge Over-Crediting](/articles/reference-free-judge-over-crediting) — **the ceiling on this page's prescription, from a higher-evidence source.** Routing review to a different model family is a bet that decorrelated graders stack. Zhou bounds that bet on a neighbouring task: three judges from three families accepting only **unanimously still pass 55%** of the errors, and Proposition 2 shows *no* monotone aggregation rule escapes, because every reference-free judge thresholds the same latent plausibility signal. So diversity of family is not the same as diversity of *what is read*, and the 6–12 points measured here are a real but bounded gain rather than the start of a curve that reaches reliability by adding reviewers. The scopes differ enough to keep both — Zhou grades exact-matchable answers where the commitment is checkable, this scores bug detection on unlabelled diffs — and the two point at the same fix from opposite ends: what buys independence is a grader that reads or commits to something the author did not, which is why the strongest single intervention on this page is *also* a commitment device (instructing GPT to target a comment count before it decides what to suppress)
- [LLM-as-a-Judge](/articles/llm-as-a-judge) — lineage bias measured on a **detection** task rather than a scoring one. The usual framing is self-preference (a judge rates its own family's output higher); on a recall task the same coupling presents as *seeing less*, which is invisible to every consistency, agreement and test-retest check because a blind reviewer is a perfectly reliable one. And here it does more than shift magnitudes — it reverses which reviewer is better, twice, on the same pair of corpora
- [Unknowns as the Agentic Bottleneck](/articles/unknowns-as-the-agentic-bottleneck) — the mechanism under that page's self-graded-quiz worry, sharpened. A quiz the model writes about its own work draws its questions from what the model thinks matters about that code; the categories it under-weights when reviewing are, on this evidence, correlated with the ones it under-weights when authoring. So a self-authored gate is not merely gradeable-into-laziness, it is **systematically silent about the author's own blind spots** before any drift starts. The 2026-07-29 research pass recorded this as an unfed gap
- [Security Debt of Agent-Generated Code](/articles/security-debt-of-agent-generated-code) — the review layer's measured detection floor (18.9% of genuine live credentials drew any comment, across seven tools including Greptile's own) with a variable it never controlled for: who authored the PR relative to which model was reviewing it. It does not rescue that floor — a 6–12 point recall difference nowhere near reaches 18.9% — but it does mean the floor is a blended figure over an unrecorded pairing, which is worth knowing before treating it as a property of automated review as such
- [Efficiency Debt of AI-Generated Code](/articles/efficiency-debt-of-ai-generated-code) — two different reasons the review dial does not work, with opposite prescriptions. Tran et al. find review time and iteration count uncorrelated with which AI inefficiencies survive and conclude the class is invisible to attention **at any depth**, so the fix moves upstream to authoring. This finds the review pass is worth 6–12 points more when a different model runs it, so the fix is lateral — change who looks, not how hard or how early. The two also give competing accounts of the same asymmetry: Tran attributes AI's authoring defects to generalist models lacking monorepo-specific context, Caridad to each model's own design instincts, and only the first has a control cohort
- [Verification as the New Bottleneck](/articles/verification-as-the-new-bottleneck) — "how far do you push automated review" gains an axis orthogonal to how far: **which model**, decided by who wrote the code
- [Post-Acceptance Edit Behavior](/articles/post-acceptance-edit-behavior) — a second axis of a model's weak read on AI-authored code, on a different task and at `empirical` tier. This page measures models failing to *catch bugs* in their own family's output; DECODE measures models failing to *anticipate what a human will change* about AI output at all — Claude Sonnet 4.6 classifies deleted/modified/unmodified at F1 **0.37** against a 0.33 random baseline, and gains nothing from being shown the developer's first four edits (0.37, 0.36, 0.35, 0.35, 0.36). Neither result is about generation capability; both say the model's read on machine-written code is worse than its write. They are not the same claim and should not be pooled — one is recall against labelled bugs, the other is prediction of human behavior
- [Deterministic Engineering for Agent Code Review](/articles/deterministic-agent-code-review) — **the recall figure this page's 52–62% collides with, 2.5× apart, and the gap is construct rather than contradiction.** OpenCodeReview's best system names 20.00% of AACR-Bench's 1,505 expert-verified comments and its best baseline 28.90%, against this page's 50.5–62.0% on ~1,500 vendor-labelled P0/P1 bugs with stylistic and documentation comments excluded — different ground truth, different scope, no population-level recall number for agent review anywhere in the corpus. What transfers both ways: this page's own finding that a recall number measured through a vendor `/review` command measures *that product's build*, not a model or an architecture, is the independent basis for OpenCodeReview's staleness objection to a Claude Code baseline pinned 33 releases behind the multi-agent `/code-review` that had shipped before the paper was submitted. And the 6–12 points measured here is the corpus's only *measured* counterweight to OpenCodeReview's own claim that a reflector's information boundary matters more than the model behind it
- [Weak-Verifier Ensembling](/articles/weak-verifier-ensembling) — the same uncited claim, restated in the lecture where it collides with its own lab's system. Asked whether generator and verifier should share a model family, Mirhoseini repeats that models "like their own generations and their own way of interpreting results much better," says she knows of no study on it — and is teaching a verifier pool built deliberately from models trained by different labs on different data. Family self-affinity would be a directional bias no weighting removes; cross-lab diversity is the right instinct against it, argued for on grounds the lecture never connects to this observation

- [Guarantees That Degrade at Deployment: Action-Space Soundness, Admissibility Without Effect, and a Vendor-Coupled Security Framework](/articles/guarantees-that-degrade-at-deployment) — this page's 6–12 point cost applied to [Ouroboros's](/articles/continuous-self-modification-under-review) all-model review panel, as a defect distinct from that gate's main one: even the admissibility judgement is degraded by shared lineage, separately from the fact that admissibility is not an effect test at all
- [DHH (David Heinemeier Hansson)](/articles/dhh) — cross-family review as standing practice (Opus/Fable authors, Codex xHigh reviews, Copilot on push), arrived at independently and explained by a mechanism that would not predict this page's result
- [Writer/Reviewer vs Agent-to-Agent Review](/articles/writer-reviewer-vs-agent-to-agent-review) — this page's crossover put to work against Claude Code's stated Writer/Reviewer rationale, which it falsifies: "fresh context (no bias toward own code)" attributes the bias to the session, and the 8.9pp measured here says it is a property of the model family that a `/clear` does not remove. The synthesis also ranks this axis first of four on evidence-per-unit-cost — it is the only one of reviewer lineage, information boundary, gate position and outcome metric with a code-review number attached

## Open Questions

- Every number here rests on a ground truth Greptile built from "sentiment analysis, upvote/downvote ratios, and git archaeology," with no protocol, no agreement statistic, no released artifact, and an LLM judge doing the matching without published validation. **Does the crossover survive on a label set someone else constructed** — a curated defect corpus with human adjudication, or an injected-bug benchmark where ground truth is golden by construction? Until then the direction is a vendor's finding and the magnitudes are uncheckable.
- Is the blindness a property of **shared training lineage** or of **stylistic fit**? The result is compatible with a much duller explanation: each reviewer happens to be strong on the bug mix the *other* agent produces, for reasons unrelated to authorship. The published category data does not settle it (composition reproduces only ~7% of the effect, which argues against the dull reading but is not a test of it). The discriminating experiment is cheap and Greptile has the datasets: run a **third-family model** — Gemini, or an open-weight reviewer — across both corpora. If lineage is the mechanism, the third model has no same-model arm and should score near the cross-model band on both. *(Bears on it, does not settle it — 2026-09-22: [AI-to-AI Code Reviews of GitHub Pull Requests](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.21311) establishes at population scale that the dull channel is real and large. With CodeRabbit held fixed as reviewer and the author varied, its comment-category mix moves 24.5pp between Claude Code- and Copilot-authored PRs across 35,248 classified comments — a pure author main effect on reviewer output, no same/cross contrast in it, since every one of those pairs is cross-product. That does not touch the crossover, which is what survives both main effects; it does mean "the two corpora simply differ" is no longer a hypothetical alternative. It cannot be the discriminating test this bullet asks for, because the study measures no recall against any ground truth and attributes at product level rather than model level — and its own future-work section proposes exactly the missing experiment: functionally equivalent PRs from known models, source-blinded, scored on defect recall and false positives across same-model, related-model and independent-model reviewers. See Closed-Loop AI Review.)*
- Caridad predicts the effect shrinks as models converge: "a year ago, the performance difference in the opening figure would likely have been larger." **Does the same protocol, rerun on the next generation of both families, show a narrower gap?** The trigger event is the next paired frontier release measured the same way; note that the prediction is also the one Greptile's own product would least like to be true.
