## Summary

As AI absorbs the *execution* of AI development ([AI Accelerating AI Development](/articles/ai-accelerating-ai-development)), the human role contracts toward a residue the essay *When AI builds itself* calls **research taste and judgment**: "choosing which problems matter, which results to trust, and when an approach is a dead end." This is the top rung of the autonomy ladder and the single capability that, if it stays human, keeps [recursive self-improvement](/articles/recursive-self-improvement) from closing the loop. Whether it stays human is the essay's load-bearing uncertainty.

## The narrowing role

The essay's clearest statement of the dynamic: "The human role is narrowing at each step in the AI development process." Two concrete narrowings:

- **Writing → reviewing.** "Once human- and AI-authored code quality reach parity, humans will stop writing code entirely, and shift to only reviewing it." (Parity is reported as *roughly now*; see [AI Accelerating AI Development](/articles/ai-accelerating-ai-development).) This is the [harness-shrinkage](/articles/harness-shrinkage-as-models-improve) story told from the human side.
- **Running → choosing experiments.** "Once Claude can run experiments, the question shifts towards 'Which of these experiments is worth running?'" The *doing* — writing the code, running the experiment, producing the result — "now costs almost nothing in human time, even if it still has costs in compute."

The residual human comparative advantage, "for now," is taste: deciding what's worth the compute. Put in the terms of [Compute Allocator](/articles/compute-allocator), the human becomes a compute allocator at the level of an entire research program rather than a single invocation.

## Why it might not stay human — "just another capability"

The essay refuses to treat research taste as a permanent human moat. Two arguments push against it:

1. **Perspiration is automatable, and that's most of the work.** Genius is "1% inspiration and 99% perspiration"; the 99% — scale it up, see what breaks, fix it, retry — is exactly what Claude excels at. Large-scale research progress "is mostly a function of tools and resources." (See [The Bitter Lesson](/articles/the-bitter-lesson), [Recursive Self-Improvement](/articles/recursive-self-improvement).)
2. **Taste shows the same capability curve as everything else.** Early evidence of improving research *judgment* — Claude beating the human next-step choice 51%→64% on hard detour moments ([AI Accelerating AI Development](/articles/ai-accelerating-ai-development)) — suggests "research taste might be just another AI capability that AI systems fail at for a time, then get good at." The precedent: AI was once bad at, then good at, explaining why a joke is funny, demonstrating theory of mind, and solving linguistic riddles — qualitative skills assumed to be human-shaped. This is the optimistic face of [Jagged Intelligence (Ghosts, Not Animals)](/articles/jagged-intelligence): today's valley is tomorrow's peak.

A frontier researcher's practitioner reading lands on the "just another capability" side. [Noam Brown](/articles/noam-brown) (OpenAI, `practitioner-opinion`) reports that models will optimize the algorithms from his poker research 10–100× but *cannot* yet invent a better one — "I can give it a lot of time and it's still not able to do it" — so today's models are "a very good complement to researchers," "not able to fully replace the whole research cycle." But he explicitly expects this to fall: "I wouldn't be surprised if at some point — same thing with coding, same thing with math — there's just this inflection point where suddenly it's actually good enough… I wouldn't be surprised if we encounter that point for research taste as well." This is [the jagged valley](/articles/jagged-intelligence) argument stated by someone watching the valley from inside a lab.

The honest counter, in an Anthropic employee's words: "The comparative advantage of humans as of right now is still in seeing the bigger picture and thinking beyond the confines of the immediate task." The question is whether "for right now" is a stable equilibrium or a receding frontier.

## The third answer: it isn't a capability at all

Both arguments above — taste as a durable moat, taste as the next jagged valley — presuppose that taste is a **capability**, something a mind either has or acquires. [Andrew Ng](/articles/andrew-ng) rejects the premise (June 2026, `practitioner-opinion`):

> "Many people describe this human contribution as 'taste,' but I prefer to think of it as humans having a **context advantage**, since that gives us a clearer path to helping AI systems get better… So long as the human knows something the AI does not, human-in-the-loop is needed to inject that knowledge into the system."

Under this reading the residue is not a faculty but an **information asymmetry** — the human knows the users, the constraints, the history, the thing so obvious nobody wrote it down. That makes the human role falsifiable (does the asymmetry exist?), perishable (it closes as context transfers), and an engineering problem rather than a mystique. It also means the question this page is organized around — *ceiling or valley?* — may be malformed. See [Context Advantage, Not Taste](/articles/context-advantage-over-taste) for the full treatment, including where the reframe fails: it explains the *deployment* asymmetry (I know my users) but not the *generative* one ([The Abstraction Barrier](/articles/abstraction-barrier), [Transformative Creativity](/articles/transformative-creativity)), where the missing thing is a concept, not a fact.

The two frames disagree about what would count as evidence. [Returns to Expertise in Agentic Coding](/articles/returns-to-expertise) is the instrument that distinguishes them: if the expertise premium declines because models acquired judgment, taste was a capability; if it declines because context transfer improved, taste was an asymmetry all along.

## The missing half: how you acquire it

Every argument above is about whether taste is *durable*. None is about whether it is *teachable* — which matters, because if the residual human role is taste and taste is innate, the role doesn't scale to the people entering it. [Jeff Dean](/articles/jeff-dean) (YC Startup School 2026, `practitioner-opinion`) is the corpus's first source to treat it as trainable and to name a protocol. He starts from the same position as Brown — asked what becomes scarce once agents write all the code, he answers "incredibly good taste in what you ask your agents to work on," and adds "I think models are not necessarily going to be that good at it" — then refuses to leave it mystical ("it's not like there's a measurable objective of taste").

Three mechanisms, in increasing order of how deliberate they are:

- **Breadth of prior problems.** The conventional answer: having worked on many things teaches you "what kinds of things might be just barely possible by cobbling together these previous approaches."
- **Score your own forecasts.** The one that is actually a protocol: "write down a bunch of things you think might be important in the next 12 months. Maybe you pick one of them to work on, but go back and evaluate in 12 months which of these other things actually seemed important, or which ones did other people in the world go out and create, and which ones did not seem to happen yet. That can give you a lot more samples for your own taste creation capability." The move is to **manufacture training data for yourself** — the counterfactual branches you didn't take get graded by the world, so one year of work yields N labeled examples instead of one.
- **Attack a load-bearing assumption on purpose.** His live example: 60 years of chip fabrication assume every chip is identical and no bit flips, while large-scale distributed systems assume the opposite and build reliability above unreliable parts — so what would you build out of transistors with 20 errors per day? He is explicit that most such experiments fail ("there are very good reasons that for the last 50 years we've done this thing this way"), which is the point: the yield is the sampling, not the hit rate.

**Why this reframes the page's central question.** If taste is a **sample-efficiency problem** — a small number of expensive, slowly-graded observations about what mattered — then it is neither a permanent moat nor an ordinary capability, and the reason models lag is legible: the training signal arrives on a 12-month delay and mostly as counterfactuals nobody records. That is a third position alongside [Ng's information asymmetry](/articles/context-advantage-over-taste) and the ceiling/valley pair, and it is the one with an obvious attack — write the predictions down where a model can read the grade. Dean does not draw that implication; it follows from his own protocol.

## The one component that got measured

Every argument above concerns a faculty nobody scores — the essay's own definition ("choosing which problems matter, which results to trust, and when an approach is a dead end") names three things, none of which has a leaderboard. **The middle one does now.** [ForecastBench](https://forecastingresearch.substack.com/p/ai-models-have-likely-reached-parity) (Forecasting Research Institute, 2026-07-16, `empirical`) scores AI systems and elite human superforecasters on the same real-world questions, resolved against the world, and the July 2026 result is that several submissions are **statistically indistinguishable from the superforecaster median** — with one, Cassi AI's pipeline, ranking above it on market questions (77.7, N=92 vs 75.9, N=56), the half of the benchmark that turns on novel one-off events rather than base-rate lookups. Superforecasting is the cleanest operationalization of calibrated judgment under uncertainty that exists, and its reference class is the strongest known human baseline, so this is the first component of "taste" the wiki can report a number on. State it narrowly, though: a p of 0.41 against a null of equal accuracy is a failure to reject rather than a demonstration of equality, the human baseline was elicited in **2024** and is extrapolated forward, and FRI both runs the benchmark and supplied the baseline. Full treatment on [Artificial Superintelligence (ASI)](/articles/artificial-superintelligence).

**The scope boundary, recorded rather than elided, because it is where the interesting disagreement lives.** Forecasting a resolvable question is not research taste, and the differences are the specific ones this page has been circling:

- **The question is given.** A ForecastBench item arrives pre-posed with pre-specified resolution criteria. "Choosing which problems matter" is the part that has been removed before scoring starts — and it is the first item in the essay's own definition.
- **The grading is free and fast.** Markets and time series resolve on a schedule; the leaderboard needs only ~225 resolved dataset questions (about 10 days) to rank a submission. That is the exact opposite of the conditions [Dean](/articles/jeff-dean) identifies as *why* taste is hard to acquire.
- **The output is a probability on a stated proposition**, not a new conceptual space. Nothing here touches the [The Abstraction Barrier](/articles/abstraction-barrier) or Boden level-3 ([Transformative Creativity](/articles/transformative-creativity)) — the generative half of the residue is untouched by a calibrated number on someone else's question.

So the honest ledger: *which results to trust* is now measured and machine parity is claimed on it; *which problems matter* and *when an approach is a dead end* are not on any leaderboard.

**The sharpest connection is to Dean's acquisition protocol, and it cuts against him.** His one actually-operational mechanism for building taste is *graded forecasting*: "write down a bunch of things you think might be important in the next 12 months… go back and evaluate in 12 months which of these other things actually seemed important." He proposes it because the labeled data is scarce — one year of work yields a handful of samples. ForecastBench is that protocol industrialized: a continuously refreshed question supply, automated resolution, thousands of scored forecasts, a public leaderboard. **The single taste mechanism on this page that was concrete enough to be a protocol is also the one that turned out to have a machine benchmark — and machines have reached the top human baseline on it.** What it says about the other two mechanisms (breadth of prior problems, deliberately attacking a load-bearing assumption) is nothing, and those are precisely the two that do not reduce to a scored proposition. The sample-efficiency framing survives the result; it just no longer implies a human advantage on the component where samples are cheap.

**It is also a datapoint for [Ng's reframe](/articles/context-advantage-over-taste), and a friendly one.** The systems at the top of this leaderboard do not win by having better judgment in the weights. Cassi's architecture is a multi-stage pipeline that generates sub-questions and search queries, retrieves up-to-date context, filters for relevance and recency, then ensembles models and has another model analyze the reasoning. That is *context acquisition as an engineering pipeline* — the information asymmetry closed by construction rather than by a faculty. If the component of taste that fell first is the one a retrieval pipeline can attack, the asymmetry reading gets the better of this particular round.

## The bottleneck either way

Even if taste stays human, it becomes the *binding constraint* — the [Amdahl's-law bottleneck](/articles/verification-as-the-new-bottleneck) of the whole pipeline. If humans can't review code as fast as Claude generates it, "human review will become the bottleneck to AI development." And if humans spend most of their time on the single-digit fraction of work that is direction-setting, each human steers vastly more work — so the *quality and throughput of human judgment* becomes the scarce resource, exactly as [Verification as the New Bottleneck](/articles/verification-as-the-new-bottleneck) predicts for verification and the HBR accountability-redesign work predicts for oversight. The risk is that the human nominally decides but actually rubber-stamps (the ["not close to substituting for senior researchers"](/articles/ai-rd-autonomy-evaluation) judgment quietly eroding into a formality).

## The human cost (the quieter thread)

The essay includes unusually candid employee quotes about what the narrowing *feels* like — worth recording because they name a cost the productivity charts don't:

- The collapse of the gift economy of small favors: asking a colleague "can you help me get this script running?" "created a little debt, a little mutual awareness. [Claude is] faster, it creates zero debt, but each of these is a lost bid for human collaboration."
- The vertigo of contingent relevance: "On days where everything works well, I can't help but think nothing I do matters … But then there are days where everything breaks and I don't understand why and I realize I have no idea what I've been up to anymore."

## Open Questions

- Is research taste a genuine ceiling (an architectural capability scaling can't reach) or the next jagged valley to fill? The essay calls this the decisive unknown. **Contested premise:** [Ng](/articles/context-advantage-over-taste) argues the question presupposes taste is a capability at all.
- If taste is automatable, what — if anything — remains a durable human comparative advantage in AI development? **Partially answered (2026-08-12), by subtraction:** one named component is off the list. Calibrated probability judgment on resolvable questions — "which results to trust" — no longer separates the strongest human reference class from a scaffolded AI pipeline on [ForecastBench](https://forecastingresearch.substack.com/p/ai-models-have-likely-reached-parity), and the pipeline that leads does it by retrieval and ensembling rather than by accumulated judgment. What remains unmeasured is the rest of the essay's own definition: choosing which problems matter (the question is supplied before scoring begins) and recognizing a dead end. The subtraction is worth more than it looks, because this was the component with the *best* claim to being measurable at all — the ones left are left partly because nobody knows how to score them.
- How do you measure rubber-stamping? "Humans set direction" can be true on paper while real judgment quietly transfers to the model.
