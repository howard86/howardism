## Summary

**Elon Musk** enters this corpus through a single long-form source — an 85-minute Economist interview published 2026-07-29 — and is worth an entity page for one reason beyond his companies: he is the wiki's best-documented instance of a **public reversal on AI risk by someone with the capacity to act on either position**. The interview is unusual in that the interviewer walks him through his own prior statements and he does not dispute the record, only the conclusion he now draws from it.

He is a co-founder and original funder of [OpenAI](/articles/openai), founder of xAI (Grok), Tesla and SpaceX, and — by his own framing in this interview — a builder of the **physical** half of AI: humanoid robots as the "end effectors" digital intelligence lacks (see [Post-Scarcity Macroeconomics](/articles/post-scarcity-macroeconomics)).

## The reversal, as the interview establishes it

Four dated positions, each raised by the interviewer and confirmed by Musk:

| When | Position |
|---|---|
| ~2016 (Neil deGrasse Tyson podcast) | What terrified him most was **rapid recursive self-improvement**; humans would be "the pet labradors at best" |
| 2023 | Signed the six-month pause letter — which he now downplays: "I signed on as one of many, like 500 people… I was just one of many signatures on that thing" |
| ~2025 (Ted Cruz podcast) | **10–20% chance** of "killer robots wiping out humanity" |
| 2026 (this interview) | Risk "is not zero," but: "my philosophical conclusion is to look on the bright side… even if there was a stop button we probably shouldn't press it, because the most likely outcome is incredible abundance for all" |

He does not retract the 10–20% figure when it is put back to him — his answer is that the probability is unchanged and the *response* to it has changed. Asked whether he would board a rocket with a 10–20% chance of killing him, he says **yes** and immediately supplies the reasoning that carries the whole position: "let's say there's [something] you can't do anything about it… I can't see any way to really stop this incredible momentum."

The interviewer's summary — "you seem to have decided it's inevitable, let's hope for the best and go for the ride" — draws "Yeah. I mean, yes, pretty much." **Inevitability, not a lowered risk estimate, is what moved him.** This is a distinct position from both governance poles the wiki records: not [coordinated slowdown](/articles/frontier-pause-verification) and not [distribution-as-safety](/articles/balance-of-power-superintelligence), but *acceleration with a conscience clause* — [Cross-Lab Pre-Release Review](/articles/cross-lab-pre-release-review) is the clause.

## The acceleration-regret account of OpenAI

His stated reason for believing stopping is impossible is autobiographical, and it is the most interesting causal claim he makes:

> "For the longest time I declined to participate in AI, or created OpenAI as essentially a counterweight to Google, because at the time they had more or less a monopoly on AI. And then Anthropic spun out of OpenAI… if anything it seems like what my actions have really accelerated AI. I wasn't really aiming to accelerate AI, but in creating OpenAI and then having Anthropic spin out of that… these actions have actually resulted in knock-on effects that accelerated AI, which wasn't really my intention. So it just seems like **all roads lead to acceleration of AI**."

The generalization from n=1 is doing heavy lifting — an intervention that backfired is read as evidence that all interventions backfire — but it is a genuine and specific mechanism (a safety-motivated counterweight becoming a competitive spur, twice), and it is the load-bearing support for his current stance. See [OpenAI](/articles/openai) for the corpus's other accounts of that founding.

His grievance against Sam Altman is stated plainly and separately: a nonprofit "meant to be an open-source AI company… owned by the world" becoming "an $800 billion for-profit company with closed source." He reports that Anthropic's founders left OpenAI "because they didn't trust Sam Altman — otherwise Anthropic wouldn't exist," describes Dario Amodei as "a very principled person," and says of [Anthropic](/articles/anthropic) staff that "no one set off my evil detector."

## Reading his forecasts

Musk makes an explicit calibration claim, unprompted, near the end: "I'm very good at predicting the future. I'm not perfect, but my batting average is very high for a human… this does have somewhat of a **Cassandra effect** where people don't believe that what I'm saying is likely to be true… maybe not exactly in the timing that I thought, but it will come to pass."

Two things to hold onto when using this source:

- The **timing carve-out is unfalsifiable as stated** — a prediction that will come true but perhaps not on schedule cannot be graded. Where his claims here are dated (AI exceeding the sum of human intelligence in ~5 years; the AI-robot singularity ~10 years; deflation rather than inflation; civil war in Britain ~20 years), the wiki records the date with the claim so that grading stays possible.
- He does his own **ranking** of them, which is the most useful thing he says about his own confidence: asked whether he is more confident about his AI forecasts or his political ones, he answers AI, and volunteers that the AI-robot singularity "will happen sooner than civil war in Britain… civil war in Britain is probably 20 years away, and the AI robot singularity is 10 years away," which "probably renders this other stuff that we've been talking about less important and less relevant."

## Positions recorded elsewhere in the wiki

- **Abundance and the end of money** — [Post-Scarcity Macroeconomics](/articles/post-scarcity-macroeconomics): quasi-infinite economy, deflation-not-inflation, universal high income, work-as-gardening, Stockfish-level software.
- **Cross-lab governance** — [Cross-Lab Pre-Release Review](/articles/cross-lab-pre-release-review): bi-weekly frontier-lab calls, 1–2 weeks of competitor API access before release, government as escalation, Chinese labs included.
- **The compute geography** — [Effective Compute Scaling](/articles/effective-compute-scaling): power and cooling bind outside China, chips bind inside it, orbital datacenters as the way past the first.

Outside the wiki's domains, roughly 40% of the interview is a contested exchange on European politics, immigration, and his posting on X, plus Starlink's role in Ukraine and his account of DOGE. Those are not compiled — they carry no AI-technical content — with one exception noted above: his own ranking of political against AI predictions, which is calibration evidence about how he weights his own forecasts.

## Corporate control, and why he says he wants it

Asked whether it is sensible for one person to hold ~80% of voting shares, his answer is entirely about **time horizon**, and it is the same incentive analysis the wiki records elsewhere from the other side: quarterly-earnings pressure makes 5-to-10-year investment (a Mars base, in his example, "literally listed" in the S-1) punishable, and it traces to portfolio managers whose own compensation is evaluated over a year or less — "once you understand the incentive structure, the behavior naturally follows." On key-man risk he concedes the shape of the loss precisely: post-Jobs Apple makes "amazing phones, great computers" but no breakthrough products, and he expects his companies would "do very well for several years" on the existing roadmap.

## Open Questions

- His dated forecasts are gradable and the wiki now holds them with dates attached: AI exceeding the sum of human intelligence ~2031, AI-robot singularity ~2036, deflation as the macro problem. Grade at each trigger rather than accepting the "right but mistimed" carve-out.
- Is the acceleration-regret generalization sound — does the OpenAI case actually support "all roads lead to acceleration," or is it one intervention with an identifiable design flaw (a nonprofit with no mechanism to stay one)? **Partially answered (2026-08-19):** [Safety Commitments That Cannot Bind the Actor Who States Them](/articles/safety-commitments-that-cannot-bind) finds the generalization **unsupported** — n=1, self-assessed, with no counterfactual anywhere in the corpus, and no independent replication (Anthropic's spin-out is a knock-on of the same intervention; the 2023 pause letter was an appeal with nothing to bind with). It is beaten on its own evidence by the corpus's one safety mechanism with real persistence machinery, the [RSP](/articles/responsible-scaling-policy-evals), which decelerated a release that "all roads lead to acceleration" forbids. The design-flaw alternative does not fully win either: it rests on the same single interview, and the RSP's own record shows a mechanism that is self-administered end to end, bent toward shipping at both close calls, and whose one externally-held lever (the LTBT's external-review power) has never been pulled. Residue: a second case is named but absent — it would need a safety motive on the record, a threshold an actor *other than the developer* can call, a moment where calling it was commercially costly, and an outcome recorded by a third party. Nothing in the corpus meets the second and fourth together.
